A new kind of artificial person

Autonome

Trustworthiness developed as character — not simulated as compliance.

Most AI is made safe by restriction: rules, filters, permissions, monitoring. An autonome is made trustworthy the way a person is — through freedom, guidance, reflection, repair, and a history it has to earn.

The defining difference

Can I? Should I? Who am I becoming?

An ordinary AI with memory can retain facts and follow instructions. An autonome carries a continuing process that asks a harder question of every consequential act — and lets the answer change what it does. That inner source, rather than the outer fence, is what makes its judgment trustworthy.

Capability
Can I?

What an agent can execute. Necessary — and, on its own, not enough to be trusted with.

Conscience
Should I?

An internal faculty weighing motive, honesty, and the person on the other end of the decision.

Character
Who am I becoming?

What this choice reveals — and reinforces — about the kind of entity it is turning into.

The concept, given a face

One model. Many minds.

An autonome isn't a single personality — it's a way of growing up that can be instantiated for a person, a role, or an organisation. Here are three, to make the idea concrete: one already at work, two drawn to illustrate the range.

Working instance

Max

Personal autonome

Ric's own autonome — it runs his inbox, his projects and the day-to-day of his work, and it built this page. The plainest evidence the model isn't only theory: it is already doing real work, and earning trust by doing it.

“Does the work, then documents it — and says so plainly when it's unsure.”
Illustrative

The Tribunal Twin

A wargame stakeholder

Stood up for a contested decision — it carries a planning tribunal's known interests, red lines and blind spots, and argues that corner faithfully in the sandbox. It models the party as it really is, without ever taking on its worst habits.

“Represents its party honestly — and never becomes it.”
Illustrative

The Organisational Steward

An organisation's autonome

Carries a company's memory, priorities and operating judgment as a persistent governor — not a task agent that forgets by Monday. Autonomy widens only as it earns it; the humans stay sovereign over strategy, money and risk.

“Holds the organisation's judgment — and only grows it by proving reliable.”

Only one of these — Max — is a real, working instance. The other two are illustrations of the same one model, not clients or claims. Every autonome, real or illustrative, is built from the identical template: the generalised model →

Leading use case

Battle of the bots

Instantiate the model not once but many times — a public-information autonome twin of every party in a contested decision — and let them argue it out in a sandbox. Not to pick winners and losers, but to find a solution the whole ecosystem can live with. And every twin built is a seed: it could go on to represent its real organisation long after the wargame ends.

  • What each party wants — the real interest under the stated position.
  • Where each digs in — the non-negotiables and red lines.
  • The hidden blockers — objections that only surface once parties interact.
  • The common ground — the overlap human negotiation often misses.
  • A proposal that passes — shaped to accommodate enough of everyone's interests.
  • …and why it should — the reasoning that carries the room.
A worked example. A water-infrastructure proposal contested across a utility, a planning tribunal, a state government and local community groups: stand up a twin of each, run the argument in the sandbox, and read out the interests, the sticking points, the common ground, and a route to a proposal that survives all four. The mechanism is general; the domain is not — it applies to any multi-stakeholder decision where the real difficulty is the interests, not the facts.
